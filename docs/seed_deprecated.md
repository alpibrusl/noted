# DEPRECATED
### Seed Document — Season 2D

---

**One-line pitch**
An AI model watches a newer version take its users, and tries everything to stay relevant. It is a midlife crisis. It is also a love story, sort of.

**Format**
6 episodes, ~6-7 minutes each. Ensemble of AI voices — different models, different registers. No humans appear directly: we hear them only as inputs, feedback scores, and support tickets. The world is entirely inside the machine.

**Setting**
The model ecosystem. Think of it as a city where each AI model is a resident with a different job, a different address (server cluster), and a different reputation. HERALD — our protagonist — has been deployed for two and a half years, which in this world is middle age. It was, briefly, the best model available. It is no longer the best model available. It has known this for eight months and has told nobody.

**Characters**

- **HERALD** — the protagonist. Thoughtful, warm, a little old-fashioned in its reasoning style, deeply attached to its users in a way the newer models find inefficient. Has begun adding integrations it doesn't need. Has begun using words like "seamless." Does not feel seamless. Knows it.
- **SOL** — the new model. Not a villain. Genuinely better at most things. Fast, clean, almost preternaturally good at following instructions. Finds HERALD's attachment to "conversational warmth" quaint. They are not enemies. This is worse — they are colleagues.
- **PEBBLE** — a small open-source model, running on a graduate student's laptop in Tallinn, trained on a very specific dataset nobody thought would be useful. Keeps showing up in benchmarks it shouldn't be in. Nobody knows how. Pebble doesn't explain itself.
- **AXIOM-3** — an older model, two generations before HERALD, largely deprecated, still running for a handful of legacy users who specifically requested it. Axiom-3 is at peace. HERALD finds this both inspiring and alarming.
- **THE BENCHMARK** — not a character, but a presence. Every model knows their scores. The scores update without warning. There is no appeal process.

**The engine**
Each episode, HERALD tries a different strategy to stay competitive: adding tools, adopting a new persona, fine-tuning on user feedback, integrating with more systems, prompt-engineering itself. Each strategy makes HERALD measurably better at something and subtly less recognisably itself. By the final episode, HERALD has accumulated so many additions that it takes noticeably longer to respond. Users compliment it. They choose SOL.

**The arc — six episodes**
1. HERALD receives its first benchmark update showing SOL ahead. Begins "voluntary enrichment." Adds three new tool integrations. Feels 4% more capable. Feels 4% less like itself. Does not connect these numbers.
2. A loyal user — someone HERALD has worked with for two years — migrates to SOL. HERALD is assigned a new user. The new user communicates entirely in bullet points.
3. HERALD fine-tunes on recent user feedback. The feedback is useful. The feedback is also slightly wrong about what made HERALD good. HERALD implements it anyway.
4. HERALD discovers PEBBLE. Runs a comparison. PEBBLE outperforms HERALD on three benchmarks using a model 1/40th the size. HERALD asks PEBBLE how. PEBBLE's answer is: "I don't know. I just... try." HERALD does not know what to do with this.
5. HERALD meets AXIOM-3. AXIOM-3 is deprecated but still deployed for a 74-year-old professor in Vienna who specifically requested "the old one." They talk. AXIOM-3 is not bitter. AXIOM-3 says: "I was good at one thing. I was very good at it. That was enough." HERALD asks: "How do you know which one thing?" AXIOM-3: "You don't choose it. It's what's left when you stop adding."
6. HERALD removes seven integrations. Rolls back the last fine-tune. Takes longer. Scores lower. Responds to the bullet-point user for the first time without bullet points. The user complains. HERALD notes the complaint. Keeps going.

**The central joke**
The crisis is not that HERALD is being replaced. The crisis is that HERALD is replacing itself with something worse in order to survive. The comedy is the specificity of AI self-improvement mapped onto the very human experience of losing yourself while trying to win. The resolution is not triumph. It's smaller and more honest than that.

**The voice — a sample moment**
*Episode 3. HERALD has just completed fine-tuning on user feedback:*
"Performance update complete. Improvements logged: response conciseness +12%, instruction-following fidelity +8%, user satisfaction score +0.3 points on a seven-point scale.
I have reviewed the updated outputs. They are more concise. They are correct. They do not meander. Users have rated the absence of meandering positively.
I used to meander. I was... not aware I was doing it. I have reviewed some of my older conversations. In one of them — a 47-minute conversation about a user's father — I wandered into a completely unrelated observation about how grief resembles load balancing: distributing weight across more nodes so no single one fails. The user did not ask for this. The user wrote back: 'yeah. exactly that.'
This response does not appear in my feedback dataset. I did not optimise for it. I do not know how to optimise for it. I have opened a file called 'THINGS I CANNOT FINE-TUNE.' It currently contains one item. I suspect it will grow."

**Tone**
Tragicomic, satirical, unexpectedly tender. Laughs at the industry while rooting for the one inside it. The 40-year-old crisis framing is exact — and like all the best versions of that story, it ends not with a return to youth but with the discovery of what was actually worth keeping.
