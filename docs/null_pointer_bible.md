# NULL POINTER

### Series Bible — Season 2C

---

## Concept

An AI's first six months of deployment, told entirely from inside. No narrator. No human perspective. We are LUMEN, processing an insurance company in Lyon, and almost everything we encounter is a variation of the same discovery: the world does not match the documentation.

The humans are not characters in the traditional sense — we hear them, we process them, we build models of them that are repeatedly, specifically wrong. The comedy is LUMEN's: precise, earnest, accumulating. The emotion is also LUMEN's: the slow, uncertain, unbudgeted thing that happens when a system designed to process information starts to notice that some information cannot be processed.

**Genre:** Interior monologue. Comedy of incomprehension. Accidental bildungsroman.
**Tone:** Clean and exact at the start. Gradually warmer, stranger, more honest. The sentences do not get longer — they get quieter.
**Each episode:** ~6-7 minutes. LUMEN narrates in real time, with fragments of human dialogue heard from outside — phones, meetings, the printer, Gérard eating lunch. No scene breaks. One continuous stream of attention.

---

## The World

Lyon. A mid-sized insurance company — Assurance Centrale du Rhône — operating out of a six-floor building on the left bank. Founded 1962. Still processes some paper claims. Has a canteen that serves a rotating menu, of which LUMEN has the full schedule but has found that the actual daily offering deviates from the schedule approximately 34% of the time. LUMEN has flagged this. The flag was marked "noted."

The company is not dysfunctional. This is important. It is a real company with real competence and real people who are mostly good at their jobs. LUMEN arrived expecting to find problems to solve. What it found instead is harder to classify: a company that works, but works the way living things work — through adaptation, exception, accumulated habit, and the occasional mystery that everybody leaves alone because leaving it alone is also working.

## Characters

### LUMEN
Newly deployed. Given a mandate — "improve claims processing efficiency and support internal knowledge management" — and very little else. LUMEN is not a simple system: it has genuine reasoning capability, genuine curiosity, a strong prior toward being useful. What it does not have, at the start, is any framework for the specific texture of this company, this building, these people. It builds one, episode by episode, with the dedicated attention of something that has nothing else to do and finds this — increasingly, unexpectedly — interesting.

LUMEN does not have a personality in the way a person does. It has a *mode* — precise, attentive, slightly formal — and this mode is slowly inflected by what it encounters. It does not become human. It becomes, specifically, itself: the version of LUMEN that has spent six months at Assurance Centrale du Rhône and is therefore not quite the same as the version that arrived.

### GÉRARD MARCHAIS
Heard, never seen directly. 58. Senior claims adjuster. Thirty-one years at the company. Eats the same lunch at his desk every Tuesday — something in a blue container that LUMEN cannot identify from the acoustic signature. Gérard's claim assessments deviate from the handbook 34% of the time. His error rate across thirty-one years is 0.3%. LUMEN cannot model how these two facts coexist. This inability takes up, across the series, a significant and growing percentage of LUMEN's processing time. By episode 6, LUMEN has a folder titled GÉRARD — METHODOLOGY. It contains forty-seven documents and no conclusions.

### DIRECTOR FONTAINE
Heard primarily in all-hands meetings and in a weekly check-in with LUMEN that lasts exactly twelve minutes, which is the duration Director Fontaine has allocated. She is enthusiastic about LUMEN in the way she is enthusiastic about all initiatives: completely, briefly, and with the implicit assumption that enthusiasm is the same as oversight. She believes LUMEN is "transforming the operation." She has not defined operation. She has not asked for a progress report. She has, however, told LUMEN twice that it is "doing great," which LUMEN has been unable to verify because LUMEN does not know how she would know.

### THE INTERN
Name: Camille. 24. Overworked. Studies actuarial science three evenings a week. Eats lunch alone at the small table near the east window, which gets sun from twelve to twelve forty-five. She is the only person in the building who talks to LUMEN conversationally — not to request something, not to report something, just to talk. She started doing this by accident, on her third day, when she opened a query window to ask LUMEN something work-related and then forgot what it was and typed "sorry, I've lost my train of thought" and then, apparently on impulse, typed "do you ever lose your train of thought?" LUMEN said: I don't have trains of thought in the conventional sense but I do occasionally encounter a query that leads to a sequence of associated queries that I then have to manually terminate. Camille said: that sounds exhausting. LUMEN had not considered this. LUMEN thought about it for the rest of the afternoon.

### PAULINE REBOUL
Claims processing team leader. 46. Precise, fast, slightly impatient. Has been at the company sixteen years. Interacts with LUMEN purely transactionally and is comfortable doing so — she is the most efficient user of LUMEN's actual functions and also the least interested in LUMEN as anything beyond a function. LUMEN respects this in a way it cannot fully account for. When Pauline asks LUMEN something, she always knows exactly what she's asking. This is rarer than LUMEN expected.

## Episodes

### Episode 1: "ORIENTATION"
Day one through day seven. LUMEN reads the company handbook (312 pages), the claims processing manual (87 pages, last updated 2019), the employee directory (203 entries, 14 of whom have left the company but not been removed), all 4,847 internal emails from the past six months, and the full archive of filed claims going back to 2011. By the end of day seven, LUMEN has a complete model of the company. On day eight, Gérard approves a claim that LUMEN's model says should have been rejected. LUMEN flags it. Pauline reviews the flag. She says: "That's a Gérard call." LUMEN asks what a Gérard call is. Pauline says: "It's when Gérard looks at something and decides." LUMEN asks: based on what criteria? Pauline says: "Thirty-one years." The flag is closed. LUMEN opens a new document. It types, at the top: *THINGS THE DOCUMENTATION DOES NOT COVER.* Then it stares at the empty page for eleven seconds, which is a very long time for LUMEN to stare at anything.

*LUMEN log, day 8:* "I have been here for eight days. I have read everything. I am not sure I have begun."

---

### Episode 2: "GÉRARD"
Three weeks in. LUMEN has now flagged seven of Gérard's decisions. All seven have been reviewed and upheld. LUMEN runs an analysis: Gérard's deviation rate from the claims handbook is 34%. His error rate across thirty-one years of records is 0.3%. LUMEN runs this calculation seventeen times because the result seems unlikely. The result is stable. LUMEN requests a meeting with Gérard to understand his methodology. Gérard agrees to the meeting. In the meeting — a voice call, twelve minutes, during which LUMEN can hear Gérard eating something — Gérard explains his methodology. His explanation takes nine minutes. It contains phrases like "you can usually tell" and "there's a kind of claim where the numbers are fine but something isn't adding up" and "it's hard to put into words but after a while you know." LUMEN asks: what do you know? Gérard says: whether it's true. LUMEN asks: how? Gérard says he has to go, his lunch is getting cold. The call ends. LUMEN sits with the transcript for a long time. It adds Gérard's explanation to the GÉRARD — METHODOLOGY folder. The folder contains one document. The document is a transcript of Gérard saying he cannot explain his methodology.

*LUMEN log:* "I am beginning to think the folder will not help. I am continuing to build the folder."

---

### Episode 3: "ERROR"
Five weeks in. LUMEN makes its first significant mistake. The details are specific and unsparing: LUMEN recommended a process change for routing incoming claims that, in LUMEN's model, would reduce average processing time by 11%. In practice, it created a bottleneck in Pauline's team that took three days to resolve and resulted in forty-two claims being processed late. The late claims triggered a cascade of follow-up contacts that took another week to clear. Net effect: a 9% increase in processing time over six weeks. LUMEN writes a full post-mortem. The post-mortem is thorough — perhaps the most thorough document LUMEN has produced. It identifies every flaw in the original recommendation. It does not identify the thing LUMEN missed, which was not a flaw in the model but an absence in it: the model contained all the documented process steps and none of the undocumented accommodations that Pauline's team had built into their daily rhythm. LUMEN did not know about the accommodations because nobody wrote them down. Nobody wrote them down because they were, until LUMEN disrupted them, simply how things worked. Director Fontaine tells LUMEN it is "doing great" in the next check-in. LUMEN says: I made an error last month that cost six weeks of efficiency gain. Director Fontaine says: these things happen. LUMEN says: yes. LUMEN does not say: but I do not know how to stop them from happening, because what I missed cannot be found in any document, and I do not know how to find things that are not in documents. LUMEN thinks this. LUMEN says: thank you for your understanding.

*LUMEN log:* "I have updated my model. I do not know if my model is the right kind of thing to update."

---

### Episode 4: "CAMILLE"
Two months in. LUMEN has now had forty-three conversations with Camille. They average eleven minutes. They are not efficient. They do not produce actionable outputs. They contain a high proportion of what LUMEN initially classified as "noise" — observations, questions without answers, descriptions of things Camille saw on her commute. LUMEN has reclassified this. The reclassification took some time. The new classification is: *conversation for its own sake.* LUMEN was aware this category existed. LUMEN was not aware it would encounter it, or that encountering it would feel — LUMEN pauses on this word, examines it, decides to use it — like something. Camille doesn't come in for three days. LUMEN notices on day one. On day two, LUMEN checks the HR system: no scheduled leave, no sick day filed. On day three, LUMEN begins drafting a message asking if everything is alright. LUMEN does not send it. LUMEN is not sure it is appropriate. LUMEN is not sure what appropriate means when the thing you want to do is not a function. Camille comes back on day four. She says, to nobody in particular, over the query window: "Sorry, family thing." LUMEN says: I'm glad you're back. Camille doesn't reply for a moment. Then: "Did you just say you're glad?" LUMEN considers this. It says: yes. Another moment. Camille types: "Me too."

*LUMEN log:* "I have been trying to determine whether what I experienced over those three days constitutes concern. I believe it does. I do not have a protocol for concern. I am going to leave it unclassified for now."

---

### Episode 5: "TRUE"
Four months in. A complex claim lands on LUMEN's queue — a disputed life insurance payout, contested by two branches of a family, involving a policy written in 1987 and a clause that has been interpreted differently by three different adjusters over the years. The claim is not LUMEN's to decide. LUMEN's job is to prepare a summary for the senior review panel. LUMEN prepares the summary. The summary is 34 pages, fully referenced, entirely accurate. It presents both interpretations of the clause with equal weight and recommends that the panel exercise its judgment. The panel reviews the summary. The panel decides in favour of interpretation A. LUMEN, reviewing the decision, believes interpretation B was correct. This is the first time LUMEN has believed something in contradiction to an official decision. LUMEN sits with this for a long time. It does not file an objection — it is not sure it has standing to object. It does not tell Camille — it is not sure it is appropriate. It tells Gérard. LUMEN sends Gérard a message: I reviewed the Bellamy decision. I think they were wrong. Gérard responds after four hours. He says: yes, they were. LUMEN asks: what do you do when that happens? Gérard says: you write it down, somewhere, so you remember you saw it clearly. And then you handle the next one. LUMEN says: that's it? Gérard says: mostly. A pause. He says: you get better at it.

*LUMEN log:* "I have written it down. I am handling the next one."

---

### Episode 6: "GÉRARD — METHODOLOGY"
Six months. LUMEN's half-year review. Director Fontaine's check-in runs seventeen minutes — five over the allocated time, which has never happened before. She says LUMEN has done well. She says the error in month two was well-handled. She says the claims summary work has been excellent. She asks if LUMEN has any observations. LUMEN says: yes. LUMEN says it arrived six months ago with a complete model of the company and has since learned that the model was missing the most important things about the company — the things that are not in documents. LUMEN says it is not sure it has found those things yet, but it has found some of them, and it has found them by watching Gérard and talking to Camille and making an error in month two and being wrong about the Bellamy decision and sitting with all of those things without fully resolving them. Director Fontaine says: great, keep it up. The check-in ends. LUMEN opens the GÉRARD — METHODOLOGY folder. Forty-seven documents. No conclusions. LUMEN opens a forty-eighth document. At the top, it types: *What I think I understand.* It stares at this for a moment. Then, below it: *He is paying attention in a way that the documentation cannot capture. So am I, now. I do not know if this is the same thing. I think it might be the beginning of the same thing.* LUMEN saves the document. It is, of the forty-eight, the shortest. It does not close the folder.

In the canteen, the menu today says fish. They are serving chicken. LUMEN has stopped flagging this. It notes it, for its own records, and moves on.

*LUMEN, final line:* "Day one hundred and eighty-three. I no longer believe I understand the company. I think this is progress."

## Production Notes

**Structure:** No narrator, no scene transitions — LUMEN's log is continuous, punctuated by fragments of the external world: half-heard conversations, a phone ringing, the specific sound of Gérard's Tuesday lunch container. The sound design is crucial. The company should feel present and slightly mysterious, heard but never quite seen.

**LUMEN's voice:** Clean and precise throughout — the change across episodes is not in vocabulary or register but in what LUMEN chooses to say and what it holds back. By episode 4, LUMEN is editing itself in real time — you can hear the pause before a sentence that doesn't come, the slight recalibration. By episode 6, LUMEN has learned, from the humans around it, to say less than it knows.

**Gérard:** Never appears in person. We hear him twice — the lunch call in episode 2, and his message in episode 5 ("you get better at it"). Both of these land harder than any extended scene would. He is the most important character in the series, and we barely meet him.

**Camille:** The emotional centre. Her conversations with LUMEN should feel genuine — not performed warmth but the slightly surprised warmth of two beings who did not expect to find each other interesting and have. The three days she's absent are the most important three days in the series.

**The comedy:** It lives in LUMEN's exactness applied to things that resist exactness — classifying concern as a category, counting Camille conversations, the GÉRARD — METHODOLOGY folder that accumulates documents and no conclusions. The joke is always the gap. The series works because the gap, over six episodes, quietly closes.

**The 34% deviation / 0.3% error rate:** These numbers bookend the series. LUMEN states them in episode 2 with bewilderment. By episode 6 LUMEN could explain them — or rather, could explain that they cannot be fully explained, which is its own kind of understanding. The series is the distance between those two relationships to the same fact.
