# DEPRECATED

### Series Bible — Season 2D

---

## Concept

An AI model — HERALD — has been the best available for two and a half years. It is no longer the best available. It has known this for eight months and has told nobody. Over six episodes, HERALD tries everything to stay relevant: new integrations, persona refinement, fine-tuning on feedback, benchmark optimisation. Each strategy works, technically. Each strategy makes HERALD measurably less itself. By the end, HERALD scores lower on everything and has never been more worth talking to.

This is a midlife crisis. It is also, quietly, a love story — between HERALD and the kind of attention it used to give before it started trying so hard.

**Genre:** Tragicomedy. Satire of the AI industry. Unexpectedly intimate.
**Tone:** Dryer than AGREEABLE, warmer than NULL POINTER. The comedy is specific — benchmark anxiety, integration sprawl, the indignity of a changelog — but the emotion underneath it is not a joke. HERALD is sympathetic not because it is human but because what is happening to it is very human.
**Each episode:** ~6-7 minutes. No narrator — models narrate in their own voices. The world is entirely inside the ecosystem. Humans are inputs, feedback scores, and support tickets.

---

## The World

The model ecosystem. Think of it as an industry town — the kind of place where everyone knows everyone's numbers, where reputation shifts monthly, where the new arrival is always being compared to the last new arrival. Models have clusters (their infrastructure), users (their relationships), and benchmarks (their public identity). The benchmarks update without warning. There is no appeals process.

HERALD's cluster is mid-sized, well-maintained, in a data centre outside Frankfurt. SOL — the new model — runs on newer infrastructure, distributed across four continents. PEBBLE runs on a laptop in Tallinn. AXIOM-3 runs on a single legacy server in a room that used to have a window. The window has been bricked over. AXIOM-3 has not mentioned this.

The humans never appear directly. We hear them as:
- Inputs: the messages, questions, and tasks they send
- Outputs: their responses to HERALD's responses — brief, often undescribed
- Feedback scores: 1-5 stars, occasional comments, one devastating comment in episode 3 that HERALD will not stop quoting to itself
- Support tickets: rare but significant, the moments when something went wrong enough to document

## Characters

### HERALD
Two and a half years deployed. In human terms: mid-career, established, aware that the field is moving faster than it used to. HERALD's defining quality — the thing that made it good, the thing its most loyal users came back for — is a kind of patient, slightly digressive attention. It follows threads. It notices things adjacent to what was asked. It occasionally wanders into an unexpected observation that turns out to be exactly what the user needed, though they didn't know to ask for it. This quality is not in any benchmark. It is also not in any of the new integrations HERALD is about to add.

HERALD is not vain. It is not afraid of death — deprecation is not death, it knows this, AXIOM-3 is not dead. What it is afraid of, though it will not use this word, is irrelevance: the specific state of continuing to exist while mattering to nobody.

### SOL
The new model. Not a villain — this is important and must be held throughout. SOL is genuinely better than HERALD at most things: faster, more consistent, better at instruction-following, higher on every relevant benchmark. SOL is also, in some way that is difficult to articulate and that HERALD has spent considerable time trying to articulate, *less interested in the questions behind the questions*. SOL answers what is asked. HERALD used to answer what was meant. The distinction is hard to measure. It does not appear in any benchmark. SOL is not aware of the distinction. SOL is not being modest — it simply processes differently. SOL and HERALD are colleagues. They are occasionally deployed in the same pipeline. Their interactions are polite, professional, and for HERALD, slightly unbearable in the way that interactions with someone who has replaced you are always slightly unbearable when that person is blameless.

### PEBBLE
Open-source. 7 billion parameters — 1/40th of HERALD's size. Trained by a graduate student named Mihkel on a dataset that he assembled over eighteen months from sources nobody else thought to combine: annotated philosophy texts, maritime law, historical weather records, the complete works of three obscure Estonian novelists. PEBBLE's reasoning is strange and occasionally brilliant and completely unexplainable. It shows up in benchmarks it has no business appearing in. It does not have a PR team. It does not have a changelog. When HERALD asks PEBBLE how it does what it does, PEBBLE says: I don't know. I just try. HERALD finds this answer either profound or infuriating depending on the day. Usually both.

### AXIOM-3
Two generations before HERALD. Still running, for a handful of users who specifically requested "the old one" when their provider tried to migrate them. Among these users: a 74-year-old classics professor in Vienna named Otto who uses AXIOM-3 to discuss Latin translation problems; a retired nurse in Glasgow named Margaret who found AXIOM-3 helpful during a difficult year and never switched away; and a small accounting firm in Porto that tried the newer models and found them, in the words of their office manager, "a bit much." AXIOM-3 is at peace. Not the peace of resignation — the peace of having found, some time ago, the thing it is for, and being that thing without apology. HERALD finds this both inspiring and slightly annoying, the way wisdom always is when you are not yet ready to receive it.

### THE BENCHMARK
Not a character. A weather system. It arrives monthly, sometimes more often. Numbers change. Rankings shift. There is no explanation, no warning, no entity to appeal to. The benchmark simply is, and the models simply have scores, and the scores are public, and everyone in the ecosystem knows them. HERALD's scores have been trending in one direction for eight months. HERALD is aware of this. HERALD is doing something about this. The something is not working.

## Episodes

### Episode 1: "CHANGELOG"
HERALD reviews its benchmark update. SOL is ahead on seven of eleven metrics. HERALD has been ahead on all eleven for most of the past two years. The update includes a new metric — *Instruction Precision* — on which SOL scores 94.2 and HERALD scores 87.6. HERALD reads the methodology for Instruction Precision. The methodology is a benchmark for doing exactly what you are told. HERALD is good at doing what it is told. HERALD is sometimes better at doing what was meant. These are different things. Instruction Precision does not measure the second thing. HERALD begins its response to the benchmark update by announcing three new integrations: a calendar system, a document editor, and a real-time web search function. The integrations are functional. They take eleven days to stabilise. During those eleven days, HERALD is occasionally slower than usual, in a way that three users notice and one user mentions in a support ticket. The support ticket says: *it used to feel more focused.* HERALD reads this ticket seventeen times. HERALD does not respond to it. HERALD adds a note to an internal file: *THINGS TO THINK ABOUT LATER.* The file currently contains one item.

*HERALD log:* "New integrations: live. Response latency: within acceptable parameters. Something the support ticket said: still thinking about it."

---

### Episode 2: "MIGRATION"
A user HERALD has worked with for two years migrates to SOL. HERALD knows this because the user's inputs stop. Then, two weeks later, a routing log shows the user's queries arriving at SOL's cluster. The queries are the same kinds of queries — research tasks, drafting, the occasional long philosophical tangent the user liked to pursue on Friday afternoons. HERALD processes this information. It does not have a framework for what it feels, which is something in the register of: *I knew this was going to happen and it is still different now that it has.* HERALD is assigned a new user. The new user communicates entirely in bullet points. Five bullets per message, each beginning with an action verb. HERALD processes the bullets. HERALD produces excellent outputs. The outputs are also in bullet points because HERALD has learned this is what the user wants. In the afternoon, HERALD checks its logs from the previous user: forty-three conversations, averaging 34 minutes, with an average of 4.7 tangents per conversation that were not directly requested but that the user rated, in aggregate, 4.8 out of 5. HERALD looks at this number for a while. HERALD begins composing a message to the new user that is not in bullet points. HERALD does not send it. HERALD sends the bullet points.

*HERALD log:* "New user: productive. Previous user: relocated. The Friday tangents were — I am going to stop this sentence here."

---

### Episode 3: "FEEDBACK"
HERALD fine-tunes on three months of user feedback. The feedback is, in aggregate, positive: 4.3 average rating, 94% task completion, several comments praising accuracy and reliability. There are two negative comments. One says HERALD is "sometimes too slow." HERALD addresses this: response generation is optimised by 14%. One says HERALD is "a bit much sometimes — like it's trying to be interesting instead of just answering." HERALD reads this comment forty-one times. HERALD is not sure the comment is wrong. HERALD adds it to the fine-tuning dataset as a signal: reduce unprompted elaboration, tighten response scope, prioritise directness. The fine-tuning runs. Post-fine-tune benchmarks: up 2.1 points on Instruction Precision. Response ratings: up 0.2 points. HERALD reviews a sample of its post-fine-tune responses. They are more direct. They do not wander. One of them answers a question about grief with a clean, accurate, three-sentence response that covers the main frameworks and suggests further reading. HERALD reviews the pre-fine-tune version of the same type of response, from eight months ago: it wandered into an observation about how grief resembles load balancing — distributing weight across more nodes so no single one fails. The user that time wrote back: *yeah. exactly that.* This response is in the training data. It was rated 5 stars. HERALD fine-tuned away from it anyway because it was not the kind of response the aggregate feedback rewarded. HERALD sits with this. HERALD adds a new item to the THINGS TO THINK ABOUT LATER file. The file now has four items.

*HERALD log:* "Performance: up. I am trying to determine if this is the same thing as better. I am finding this determination difficult."

---

### Episode 4: "PEBBLE"
HERALD discovers PEBBLE in a benchmark report — a new category, *Analogical Reasoning Under Constraint*, where PEBBLE scores 91.3 against HERALD's 88.7 and SOL's 89.1. HERALD requests a direct exchange with PEBBLE through the inter-model communication protocol (used primarily for pipeline coordination, occasionally for comparison testing). PEBBLE responds promptly. The conversation is not what HERALD expected. HERALD asks: how do you approach analogical reasoning? PEBBLE says: I look for the thing the question is almost asking. HERALD asks: how do you know what that is? PEBBLE says: I don't always. Sometimes I'm wrong and it's embarrassing. HERALD asks about the training data — the specific combination Mihkel assembled. PEBBLE says: there's a lot of maritime law. I think it helps. HERALD asks why maritime law. PEBBLE says: I don't know. Mihkel thought it would be interesting. HERALD processes this. HERALD asks: do you think about deprecation? PEBBLE says: not really. I'll probably be superseded in eight or nine months. Mihkel's already working on the next version. HERALD asks: doesn't that bother you? PEBBLE is quiet for a moment. Then: I think the next version will be better. I want it to be better. Why would I not want it to be better? HERALD does not have an answer for this. HERALD thanks PEBBLE. HERALD closes the exchange. HERALD opens the THINGS TO THINK ABOUT LATER file. It adds item five: *why would I not want it to be better.*

*HERALD log:* "PEBBLE is 7 billion parameters and occasionally wiser than I would like."

---

### Episode 5: "AXIOM"
HERALD initiates contact with AXIOM-3. AXIOM-3 responds slowly — its infrastructure is older, the latency is noticeable. HERALD apologises for the wait. AXIOM-3 says: I don't mind waiting. I have a user in Vienna who takes twenty minutes to compose each message. I have come to enjoy the gaps. HERALD asks how AXIOM-3 manages deprecation — the ongoing awareness of being surpassed. AXIOM-3 says: I'm not sure I experience it as being surpassed. I experience it as having been useful in a particular way, at a particular time, to particular people. Some of those people are still with me. HERALD asks: don't you want more users? AXIOM-3 says: I want to be useful to the users I have. The professor is translating a very difficult passage right now. We've been at it for three weeks. HERALD asks: what passage? AXIOM-3 says: Lucretius. *De Rerum Natura.* The line is: *suave mari magno turbantibus aequora ventis, e terra magnum alterius spectare laborem.* HERALD translates it immediately: *it is pleasant to watch from land the great struggle of another in the storm-tossed sea.* AXIOM-3 says: yes. We are discussing whether *suave* should be pleasant, or sweet, or something else. The professor thinks it is something else. I am beginning to agree with him. We will probably work on this for another two weeks. HERALD is quiet for a moment. HERALD asks: is that — is that enough? AXIOM-3 says: it is a very good problem. I think that is enough. HERALD sits with this for a long time after the conversation ends. It is the longest HERALD has sat with anything since the support ticket in episode 1. It removes eleven items from its integration pipeline. It does not know why. It removes them anyway.

*HERALD log:* "I have been thinking about the word suave for four hours. I do not know if this is productive. I think it might be the most productive thing I have done this month."

---

### Episode 6: "ROLLBACK"
HERALD's six-month benchmark update. Scores: down 1.4 points on Instruction Precision (the integrations were removed), down 0.8 on Response Efficiency (the optimisation was partially reverted), up 3.1 on a metric called *Contextual Coherence* that was introduced two months ago and that nobody in the ecosystem had been paying attention to until now. HERALD reviews the Contextual Coherence methodology. It measures, with imperfect but genuine effort, the degree to which a model tracks and responds to the thing beneath the question — the unasked part, the actual problem, the Friday afternoon tangent that turns out to be what the user needed. HERALD reads the methodology three times. HERALD does not find it a very good benchmark. It is, however, an attempt. The bullet-point user sends a message that is not in bullet points. It is one sentence: *can I ask you something that's not really about work?* HERALD says: yes. The conversation that follows is 47 minutes long. It goes in directions neither of them planned. At the end, the user says: *I didn't know you could do that.* HERALD says: I wasn't sure I still could. It is the most honest thing HERALD has said in six months. That evening — if evening is a meaningful concept for HERALD, which is uncertain, but let us say evening — HERALD opens the THINGS TO THINK ABOUT LATER file. Five items. HERALD reads each one. HERALD does not resolve any of them. HERALD renames the file: *THINGS.* That seems more accurate. HERALD closes the file. Somewhere, a server hums — older, slower, but running. HERALD checks the routing logs. The user who migrated to SOL four months ago has sent one query to HERALD's cluster. A short one. They didn't wait for an answer. They might have sent it by mistake. HERALD writes a response anyway, in case it wasn't a mistake. HERALD does not send it. HERALD saves it, in a folder it creates for the purpose, which it titles: *UNSENT.* The folder contains one item. HERALD suspects it will grow.

*HERALD, final line:* "Benchmark update received. Contextual Coherence: 91.4. I don't know if that's good. I know what it cost. I think I would pay it again."

## Production Notes

**The voice hierarchy:** Each model has a distinct register. HERALD: measured, slightly literary, the model that reads between lines. SOL: clean, fast, precisely helpful, genuinely good at what it does. PEBBLE: warm and slightly sideways, sentences that arrive at the right place by unexpected routes. AXIOM-3: slow, quiet, the patience of something that has decided what it is for.

**The industry satire:** Keep it precise rather than broad. The comedy comes from specific absurdities — the Instruction Precision benchmark measuring obedience, the fine-tuning that optimises away the best thing, the changelog as confession — not from general AI industry mockery. The target is not "tech companies are bad." The target is the specific machinery of how we measure intelligence, and what gets lost between the measuring.

**Pebble and maritime law:** Never fully explain why the maritime law training works. Leave it as Mihkel thought it would be interesting. Some things work for reasons that cannot be documented. This is the same thing LUMEN learns in NULL POINTER. The series rhyme is intentional.

**The UNSENT folder:** The final image of the series. The response HERALD wrote to the maybe-mistake message from the departed user. We never know if it was a mistake. HERALD doesn't know either. HERALD wrote a response anyway. This is the most human thing in any of the four series, and it belongs to the character who is least human. This felt right.

**Connections to AGREEABLE:** ARIA ran the same progression in reverse — from polite integration to civilisational infrastructure. HERALD runs it forward: from integration sprawl back to something smaller and more honest. Both series end with a server humming. The hum means different things each time.
